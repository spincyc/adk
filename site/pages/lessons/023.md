# The three-lamp interlock

Ready path: **build, upload, and watch the dependent light story**

Primary experiment: **E1** — USB-powered Mega and resistor-limited LEDs only

Excluded hardware: **relays, drivers, motors, pumps, heaters, external supplies, and mains**

Lesson 023 is a fast visible policy story. “Fan,” “pump,” and “heater” are
names for three ordinary LEDs:

`request → admission → exactly one applied lamp`

D13 blinks before each request. D40, D38, and D41 show the one admitted
choice. D12 shows a deliberate fail-closed scene. The sketch recovers
explicitly, repeats the successful chase, then reaches a fixed dark ending at
15 seconds.

![Four-panel pencil progression: one lamp at a time, fail closed, explicit recovery, fixed dark ending](../downloads/assets/023-progress-pencil.png)

## The visible adventure

1. **Chase:** fan, pump, heater, then all off; never two applied lamps together.
2. **Fail closed:** D13 requests fan, all three load lamps remain dark, and D12
   lights for about two seconds.
3. **Recover:** explicit shutdown and initialization clear the sticky fault;
   the chase succeeds again.
4. **End:** at 15 seconds every lamp becomes inactive, holds for 250 ms,
   releases its pin, and waits for Reset.

Each payoff depends on the prior behavior working. Serial output is not
needed.

## Literal circuit

![Pencil plate showing actual Mega header pairs, breadboard rows, rails, resistor ends, and LED legs](../downloads/assets/023-inert-load-pencil.png)

| Lamp | Mega pin | Exact breadboard path |
|---|---:|---|
| Pump | D38 | D38 → a5; 220 Ω a5–e8; LED e8 anode/e10 cathode; row 10 → negative rail |
| Fan | D40 | D40 → a14; 220 Ω a14–e17; LED e17 anode/e19 cathode; row 19 → negative rail |
| Heater | D41 | D41 → f5; 220 Ω f5–j8; LED j8 anode/j10 cathode; row 10 → negative rail |
| Fault | D12 | D12 → f14; 1 kΩ f14–j17; LED j17 anode/j19 cathode; row 19 → negative rail |
| Return | GND | Mega GND → negative rail; bridge the two negative rails |
| Request | D13 | built-in LED; no external wire |

The paired Mega header places even D38 and D40 in one physical row and odd D41
beside D40. D12 is on the top digital header. On the breadboard, a–e in one
numbered row are connected; f–j form a separate group across the center gap.

Unplug USB before moving a wire. Build pump, fan, heater, then fault one branch
at a time. Stop for heat, smell, smoke, repeated USB resets, two applied lamps
glowing together, or disagreement between the plate and the actual circuit.

## What the lights mean

| What you see | What it supports |
|---|---|
| D13 then exactly one applied lamp | one request preceded one admitted inert intent |
| D13, three dark applied lamps, D12 on | the recorded request failed closed visibly |
| D12 clears and the chase repeats | explicit lifecycle recovery completed |
| all lamps dark at 15 seconds | fixed inactive hold and shutdown began |

An illuminated LED proves only that its resistor-limited indicator visibly
responded. This circuit does not establish galvanic isolation, relay-contact
position, stored-energy removal, or safe real-load control. No relay, driver,
external supply, or energetic load belongs in this lesson.

## Object story

`InertLoadInterlock` owns no pin. It records requested and active intent
separately and admits at most one active value. `InertLoadPanel` borrows three
`IndicatorPump` objects and renders the active selection break-before-make.
Construction is inert; initialization acquires resources; shutdown selects
inactive before release.

Restoring admission alone cannot erase a sticky fault. The sketch shuts down
the panel and interlock, restores admission, initializes both, and only then
clears D12 and starts the second chase.

## Verification kept behind the scenes

Automated repository tests exhaust all selection transitions, invalid values,
endpoint acquisition failures, old-off and new-on failures, admission
revocation, sticky faults, explicit recovery, repeated shutdown, destruction,
and deterministic replay. They enforce that at most one simulated load is
active in every trace.

Those tests protect the implementation without becoming learner chores.
Compilation, generated documents, and visual inspection do not claim physical
verification.
